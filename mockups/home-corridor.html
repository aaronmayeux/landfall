The corridor chart — on a real major hurricane

The SVGs below are generated by ui/chart-home.js, the shipping code, not redrawn for this page. Zero is the cyan line at the top and the storm rises toward it, so the closest approach is a summit rather than the bottom of a V. Each wind band grows up from the centre track and closes the gap; where one touches the home line, that line wears its colour for the hours the wind is on the house. The dashed amber is the earliest arrival allowing for track error — ours, not NHC's. Every case here is a real storm against a real house. The fabricated Cat 3 that used to stand in for a hurricane has been retired; Ida covers what it was standing in for, and covers it with published numbers.

What to judge. Does the inverted axis still read when the storm passes almost overhead and everything compresses against the ceiling — or do you catch yourself re-checking which way is closer? Do three translucent bands stay legible on a phone in daylight, or turn to mud? Is the coloured segment on the home line strong enough to carry "the wind is on your house right now", given the band above it is clamped and cannot show depth — and does it survive shrinking to the 50-minute stripe in the middle panel? And does the dashed amber read as a hedge rather than as a second forecast?

The band is 2021's, not 2026's. NHC republishes the cone circle radii every spring off the previous five years, so Ida is measured against the table that was in force during her own season — 40 nm at 24 h where 2026 says 39, and 55 nm at 36 h where 2026 says 49. The figures on this page name the season they came from.
